# Materials and methods – proposed text (statistics, software, figures)

## 2.x Data and preprocessing
We analysed the Romanian records of the ECHO citizen-science network as exported from the open ECHOREPO repository (n = 1,091 samples from 444 collectors, November 2025 – October 2026). Each record contains the collector identifier, the date of upload, geographic coordinates and six field indicators: soil pH (colorimetric kit, 0.5-unit steps, range 3–9), organic matter (colour-chart class: 1.5, 2, 2.5, 3.5 or 5), number of earthworms, number of pollutant items (counts), soil structure (friable, intact, firm, compact, high compact; treated as nominal) and soil texture (eight classes; the class "other" was excluded from texture analyses). Samples were taken from the 0–30 cm layer and homogenised before assessment. To protect privacy, the data provider displaced each coordinate by a fixed random offset of up to 1 km; true locations are not available. Reported distances between two samples may therefore deviate by up to 2 km. Pairs of samples closer than 2 km were consequently excluded from all spatial statistics, and distances were analysed in bands rather than as continuous values. The time stamp corresponds to upload; only the calendar day was used. No values of the original file were altered. Six missing organic-matter values (0.5%) were completed with the mean of the three nearest samples with observed values, rounded to the nearest existing class; this version was used only in a sensitivity analysis, because neighbour-based imputation introduces spatial dependence, and all inferential spatial analyses relied on complete cases.

## 2.x Characterisation of the network and data quality
The concentration of contributions among collectors was described by the Gini coefficient (with a percentile bootstrap interval obtained by resampling collectors), the Lorenz curve and Hill numbers of order 0, 1 and 2 (effective numbers of contributors) (Hill, 1973). The proportion of collectors with a single sample was reported with a Wilson interval (Wilson, 1927). Prevalences of selected classes were given with Wilson intervals and with percentile cluster-bootstrap intervals (4,000 replicates) in which collectors were resampled with replacement (Efron, 1979), which accounts for the non-independence of samples collected by the same person.
Digit preference in pH readings was quantified as the ratio between the observed frequency of whole-unit values and the mean frequency of the two adjacent half-unit values (1 = no preference), with a cluster-bootstrap interval; differences among collectors with at least ten samples were tested with a chi-square statistic whose null distribution was obtained by permutation. Earthworm counts were examined for over-dispersion with the Poisson dispersion test (Cameron & Trivedi, 1990) and fitted with an intercept-only negative binomial model, comparing observed and expected numbers of zeros. Heaping at multiples of five was evaluated against the geometric interpolation of neighbouring counts and is reported as exploratory. The plausibility of very low pH values (≤ 3.5) was checked by comparing the median pH of their five nearest eligible neighbours (≥ 2 km away, different collector) with that of samples with pH 4.0–4.5 (Mann–Whitney U test).

## 2.x Spatial autocorrelation
Spatial dependence of the ordinal and count variables (pH, organic matter, earthworm counts) was measured with Moran's I (Moran, 1950; Anselin, 1995) calculated on mid-ranks, using binary distance-band weights (2–5, 5–10, 10–20, 20–50, 50–100 and 100–200 km) that were row-standardised. For nominal variables (soil structure, soil texture, presence of earthworms, presence of pollutants) we computed the mean proportion of neighbours in the same class and expressed it as a chance-corrected spatial kappa, (observed − expected)/(1 − expected), in the spirit of join-count statistics (Cliff & Ord, 1981) and Cohen's kappa (Cohen, 1960). Each statistic was computed for all pairs and for pairs of samples from different collectors only.
Significance was assessed by Monte-Carlo permutation (2,999 permutations; two-sided p = (1 + number of equal or more extreme values)/(1 + 2,999)). Three null models were used: (i) values permuted across the whole country, which tests any spatial structure including the regional gradient; (ii) and (iii) values permuted only within 50-km or 100-km grid blocks, which retain the regional gradient and test whether structure exists at scales smaller than the block (Besag & Diggle, 1977; Legendre, 1993). P-values were adjusted for multiple testing within each null model with the Benjamini–Hochberg false-discovery-rate procedure (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995).

## 2.x Neighbour consistency and collector effects
Consistency between neighbouring samples was expressed as the Spearman rank correlation between the value of each sample and the median value of its k nearest neighbours (k = 3, 5, 10). We contrasted a naive version, in which all other samples were eligible neighbours, with a corrected version in which neighbours had to be at least 2 km (or 5 km) away and had to have been collected by a different person. Because neighbourhoods overlap, interval estimates were obtained with two bootstrap schemes (1,500 replicates): resampling collectors and resampling 100-km spatial blocks; the latter is the more conservative. Permutation tests (1,500 permutations; k = 5, 2 km) compared the observed correlation with that obtained by shuffling values globally and within 100-km blocks.
The contribution of the collector and of place to the variation of each indicator was estimated with a crossed random-effects model fitted by restricted maximum likelihood to rank-based inverse-normal scores (Blom transformation): y = μ + collector + 25-km cell + 100-km block + residual. Variance shares were computed from the estimated components, and each random effect was tested with a likelihood-ratio test using the 50:50 mixture of χ²(0) and χ²(1) distributions appropriate for variances on the boundary of the parameter space (Self & Liang, 1987; Stram & Lee, 1994). One-way intraclass correlation coefficients, ICC(1), on ranks (Shrout & Fleiss, 1979) with collectors, 10-km cells and 25-km cells as groups, and Kruskal–Wallis tests among collectors with at least ten samples, are reported as descriptive complements. Because collectors usually sample close to home, collector and place effects are partly confounded; we therefore also tested the collector effect conditionally on place: among pairs of samples 2–25 km apart within the same 25-km cell, the mean dissimilarity of pairs from the same collector was compared with that of pairs from different collectors (absolute rank difference for ordinal variables, mismatch for nominal variables), with a null distribution obtained by permuting collector labels within cells.

## 2.x Software, reproducibility and figures
All analyses were run in Python 3.13 with NumPy 2.5.3, pandas 3.0.5, SciPy 1.18.1 and statsmodels 0.15.0 (Seabold & Perktold, 2010). Every random procedure used a seeded pseudo-random number generator (NumPy PCG64) with a distinct seed per analysis module; the complete code, the input file (SHA-256 checksum recorded), the derived data, all result tables and figures are available at [repository DOI to be added] and can be regenerated with a single command (python scripts/run_all.py). Figures were produced exclusively with Matplotlib 3.11.2 (Hunter, 2007), with a common minimalist style: sans-serif type (Arial, or the metrically compatible Liberation Sans), no chart junk, light horizontal grid lines, and panel labels in bold lower-case letters. The three-colour palette (blue #3556a8, teal #16a085, ochre #e6a519) was checked for colour-vision deficiency (minimum adjacent OKLab ΔE ≥ 12), and every series is additionally encoded by marker shape, line style or hatching, so that the figures remain legible in black-and-white print. Figures are exported as vector PDF with embedded TrueType fonts and as 300-dpi PNG at single- (85 mm) or double-column (180 mm) width.
